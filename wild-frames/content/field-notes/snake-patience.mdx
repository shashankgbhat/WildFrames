There is a particular quality of light that arrives only after you have stopped moving.

Not just the light itself — the angle, the temperature — but what the light lands on. A snake that was coiled and alert ten minutes ago is now stretched along a rock, jaw resting against warm stone, eyes half-closed. The frame you wanted at the start of the encounter was tense. This one is honest.

## The waiting is the technique

Most of the craft advice in wildlife photography focuses on the camera: aperture, shutter speed, focal length. These matter. But the photograph that changes how someone sees a species is usually made after the photographer has done something less glamorous — stayed still long enough for the subject to forget they are there.

Reptiles are especially responsive to this. Their nervous system is tuned for movement detection. A slow approach followed by genuine stillness communicates something closer to what a rock or a log communicates than what a predator does. The animal's posture changes. The photograph changes with it.

## What patience actually looks like

In the field, patience is often uncomfortable. You are crouched in an awkward position. The light is shifting. There are insects. The shot you imagined is not materializing.

The discipline is staying anyway — not because the original shot is coming, but because you do not know yet what the animal will do. The best frames I have made were not planned. They were residue from enough time in one place.

## A note on ethics

Patience is also the most ethical approach. A rushed encounter stresses the animal and produces inferior photographs. Time spent waiting at a respectful distance allows behavior to emerge naturally rather than being compressed by anxiety or flight.

The photograph that results from a patient encounter tends to look like it, even if the viewer cannot say exactly why.
